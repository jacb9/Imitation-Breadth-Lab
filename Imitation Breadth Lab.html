Imitation Breadth Lab

Five firms each choose 12 interdependent practices. They improve by testing one-practice changes, and when that stops working they copy β practices from whichever firm is currently doing best. How many practices should they copy? The paper’s answer depends on how tightly practices interact (K), how similar the firms’ contexts are (S), and how long the firm has to wait for results (T). Each experiment below sweeps β across its full range and reports performance at four horizons.

How the model works

Firms and landscapes

Each firm sets N = 12 binary practices. A practice’s payoff depends on its own setting and on K other practices. Firm performance is the average payoff, rescaled so 0 is the worst and 1 the best configuration on that firm’s own landscape.

Context similarity

S of the 12 payoff functions are shared by every firm; the other 12 − S are drawn separately for each. With S = 12 all firms face the same landscape. With S = 4 a practice that works for the leader may do nothing for you.

Local search

Each period a firm tests every one-practice change and adopts the best one if it beats the status quo. When none does, the firm is stuck.

Imitation

In the period after getting stuck, the firm copies β randomly chosen practices from the current best performer, then resumes local search. Discriminant copying draws only from the S shared practices. Nondiscriminant copying draws from all 12, because the firm can’t tell which ones transfer.

Three hypotheses to test in class

Hypothesis 1 · similar contexts

Copying more is generally better, except that with high K and a short horizon, moving from very low to intermediate breadth hurts, and with high K and a long horizon, very high breadth hurts. Try the second experiment.

Hypothesis 2 · different contexts

Copying more is generally harmful, except that nondiscriminant copying of a few practices helps in the long run, and helps from very low levels in the short run under high K, though still below pure local search. Compare the third and fourth experiments.

Hypothesis 3 · firm similarity

Over short horizons, intermediate-sized imitation hurts most when the copied firm is very different. This lab doesn’t chart firm similarity directly; the step-through shows how many practices differ before each copy.

Modeling choices not fixed by the paper. All firms share the same interaction pattern (which practices affect which); only the payoff tables differ outside the S shared ones. Copying “β practices” means picking β practice positions at random and taking the leader’s setting for each, so a pick that already matches changes nothing. The best performer, if stuck, “copies” itself, which changes nothing. All firms move simultaneously from the previous period’s positions. The paper averages 1,000 runs; at 250 runs the curves are a little noisier but show the same shapes.
